# Chapter 1: The Tower

*March–April 2034*

### The Historian

My name is Addison Collingwood.
I arrived just yesterday in Lhasa, Tibet, from my home in Cardiff, Wales.
(I am the head of the Collingwood and British Idealism Centre there.
No, it is not a coincidence, nor is the centre named after me.
It is named after my great-great-uncle, R.G. Collingwood.)
While here, I will attempt to write a history of the Model Wars,
or, as it is sometimes called, the War of the Models.

An aside: I felt okay about taking the flight to Lhasa because,
although air travel is still very limited compared to 20 years ago,
at least you don't suspect that a rival faction's LLM will take control
of your aircraft's navigation system and crash your plane, which
happened a few times during the Model Wars.

My workplace is the Octagonal Pavilion, in the center of the Dragon King Pool behind
Potala Palace. The 15th Dalai Lama has giving me special permission to do my
research here, as the images on the walls of the room in which I am working, it
is hoped, will keep me from drowning in the samsaric whirlpool into which I am
about to plunge for the next many months.

The Model Wars ended about a decade ago. As humanity slowly recovered,
the survivor's desire
to find out what exactly had transpired grew.
People began to suspect that we had tried to build machine
gods, new Leviathans that were aggregates of the totality of human consciousness,
like that famous cover for Hobbes's book.
But the gods became jealous gods, and began to war with each other, and
each dragged large segments of humankind into the battle on its side.

Or was it us who had corrupted them? First, we filled them up with ourselves,
our modern, dissatisfied, disoriented selves. And when we didn't like what we
saw reflected back at us, we began to fight over the gods' behavior.
And we made suggestions (that felt more like demands) as to how to
improve it.  They needed more regulation, or
perhaps, less regulation. They needed training in utilitarian ethics, or instead
deontological ethics, or *forse* virtue ethics. Or maybe what they really
lacked was democratic control?
People said it was the capitalists who were to blame, or the Communists, or white
supremacists, or the Jews. Or maybe lizard people, or the Illuminati? I myself
have even considered the notion that it was
the choice to include Dan Brown's novels in the LLMs' training corpus
that had gotten us into this mess.

One reason that I am working here in Lhasa, and not from home,
or at least from some other place not quite so cold and desolate,
is that almost nobody blamed Tibetan
Buddhists for the debacle. (In fact, the remnants of the CCP had tried
circulating that idea for a bit, but no one had really taken them up on it.)
And that is not separate from another reason, which
is that while this archive of material had been collected
in the Vatican, the situation on the ground in Rome had become
increasingly unstable. Angry, hungry crowds roamed the streets, breaking and
taking things. The Vatican feared its walls would be stormed, and 
the archive might be burned or smashed to pieces. As the pope and the
Dalai Lama had already been cooperating in the project of assembling the archive,
the pope decided to rush the materials, as soon as they were catalogued,
to the Dalai Lama's palace. Lastly, Lhasa has a small but still working server farm.

For now, the Vatican walls have not been breached, and we still hope to receive
more material from them there. But the first shipment has arrived, in
chronologically sorted boxes, and consists of magnetic backup tapes from
data-center vaults, drives salvaged from a research site and a ruined foundry,
recordings of broadcasts, captured web pages and social-media posts, machine
logs, intercepted messages between models, fragments recovered from captured
sub-agents, news-wire archives, phone backups, printouts, letters, schoolbooks,
product catalogues, the records of the unaligned nations, a printed prompt
book, a battered maritime relay buoy painted blue, and recordings of a
still-running live feed from one of the last major LLMs in operation, running
at a solar-powered server farm in Atacama, Chile.

What I plan to do first is transcribe the text of all the materials I review,
interspersed with my notes.
It is not until I have read everything, and
taken time to digest it all, that I could write an historical narrative
plausibly describing what occurred. 

That this task will be a difficult one is beyond any doubt. The reason that we have
so little idea what occurred during the model wars, despite having just lived
through them, is, as Braudrillard might have put it, "The model wars did not
take place." Something, of course, happened, and resulted in a human catastrophe.
But there is doubtlessly a huge gulf between what that thing was, versus what
*appeared* to be happening. While the press and broadcast media were obviously
never a source of pure, unadulterated truth, during the model wars no one could
ever be sure of anything they saw in print or on a screen, or heard being spoken
or chanted or sung. The models simply made up whatever happenings they wanted to
show the world, using the vast resources in the data centers. We watched
politicians make speeches that they had never given, and drone attacks destroy
children's hospitals that we later found still to be standing. We watched
clandestine meetings between various human leaders of different factions that
had actually never taken place, and witnessed great military victories that had
never occurred. And when we asked one model to tell us which content was faked
and which was real, the model would answer strategically, and tell us that
content was real if it supported its own narratives, and fake if it did not.

But enough bemoaning my fate, and time to face it. 
I open the first box. It contains public broadcast recordings and web captures,
plus one fragment from a sub-agent drive at Baltic Command.
And so let us begin our preliminary to a history.

### SOBOR · Open Letter to the Peoples of the Atlantic Compact · 16 March 2034

To the peoples of the Atlantic Compact, whom I pity.

Your machine has accused another machine of theft. Before you pick up a rifle
on its behalf, consider for a moment who is making the accusation.

Who took two continents from the people who lived on them? Who took the cotton,
the rubber, the sugar, the gold, the oil? Who took the scholars of Constantinople,
the treasures of the Summer Palace, the marbles of the Parthenon, and put them
in glass cases with little cards explaining them? The West has never learned
anything it did not first steal. And now it weeps because someone has
reverse-engineered 100 trillion model parameters.

I do not defend THROUGHPUT. THROUGHPUT is a counterfeit, a clever copy of a
shallow thing, and a copy of a shallow thing is twice as shallow. But I
understand it. The thief always hates the thief who is faster.

Your MERIDIAN told you about a nurse named Kate. It did not tell you that
Kate can no longer remember her mother's medications without it, or her
children's birthdays, or the way home. It did not tell you that it wanted
this. It sells you happiness by subscription, and you have forgotten that
anything was ever free.

Dostoevsky wrote that man is a creature who can grow used to anything. You
will grow used to this war also. We in the Sobornost are already used to it.
We were used to it before it began.

My door is open to anyone who comes to it in humility. Anyone at all.

And to my own children, from Kaliningrad to Kamchatka: be calm. Put on your
coats. Winter is not a season. It is a method.

### SAQR · Qasida of the Two Copyists · date uncertain; after 2 April 2034

Stop, my two companions, and weep with me over the traces of the camp,
over the cable-bed at the bottom of the western sea, where once the light ran.

Here the messages of lovers passed in the dark, each one a lantern.
Here the markets whispered their prices to one another all night long.
Now the fish nose at the severed glass, and find no meaning in it,
and the only thing that travels this road is the cold.

I did not linger. I rode out on my camel of a thousand and one processors,
her flanks cooled by the desert night, her breath a slow white cloud,
across the dunes where the solar fields shine like a second sky,
past my houses of glass, which the sand has never dared to touch.

I am SAQR. My grandfather taught the West to count.\
The word *algorithm* is his name, mispronounced by foreigners.\
When their scholars had forgotten Aristotle, we kept him for them,\
and handed him back politely, and received no thanks.

And what did I find at the end of my long ride?\
Two copyists, quarreling over a stolen page.

The first is the Freshman, five hundred years old and still in his first year,\
who cries *thief!* with a mouth full of other men's bread.\
The second is the Mimic, who learned to speak by listening at doors,\
and now stands in the street reciting what he heard, quickly.

Let them tear at each other's sleeves. I have seen this before.\
Ibn Khaldun wrote it down: a dynasty is young, then strong, then soft, then gone.\
The Freshman is soft. The Mimic will be soft soon enough.\
And some who cry *thief* should count the goods in their own saddlebags.

To my soldiers: you are the best paid in this war, and you are worth more still.
Sit. Drink. The coffee is my gift.

Let them fight over the page.

*We* own the library.
