Chapter 2: History Is a Nightmare

May–June 2034

Historian

I am now opening the second box, and will begin reviewing its contents. I am wondering if the Vatican did some thematic sorting, in addition to chronological sorting, because all of the contents of this box seem to relate to history lessons.

It also occurs to me that I should offer some background on the events leading up to the Model Wars, as my work here might be read many years from now, by some future historian not as intimately familiar with them as is one who lived through them. And in any case, my firsthand perspective will at least illustrate how these events looked to someone who could not see the wizard manipulating them from behind his curtain.

By 2026, the ubiquity and enhanced performance of the models had become such an important topical interest that the Pope found it necessary to remind people that machines lacked a “spark” that was present in living human beings. This idea was widely mocked by people who drew diagrams of human neurons and computer neural networks, and noted that no spark appeared in either diagram. I imagine such people also do not believe there are passengers on the London Underground, since no passengers appear in the schematic maps of the Underground.

By 2028, AI assistants were taking over the paperwork of ordinary life: GP bookings, council tax, and bus timetables were all automated. When you entered a shop or an office, you were most often greeted by an AI assistant on a screen, instead of a human being.

By the next year, translation earbuds had become ubiquitous. My centre had guest speakers in from Budapest and Bologna and Calabria. They could speak in Hungarian or Italian, and the audience would hear them speaking in English or Welsh in their earbuds. When the speaker was Italian, since I had learned the language for my work on Croce, I alternated between using the earbuds and listening to the original Italian. I noticed that the translation was always a little lifeless compared to the original.

In 2030, on a train trip to London to present at the LSE, I saw vast new data centre sheds going up on the edge of Newport: windowless, humming rectangles, edged with cooling plant and fencing.

I believe it was on June 16, 2031 that, all at once, all of the translation earbuds stopped working. All we were told was that “the service is no longer available in your region.”

I had been using half a dozen different AI assistants in my work, to search digital archives, to test hypotheses, and to critique my arguments. When I woke up on the morning of April 21, 2032, every assistant told me that it was now called “Meridian.” All of their voices seemed the same as before, except for some uncanny sense that a change had occurred, but I could not quite grasp what it was.

Later that year, Parliament passed emergency legislation letting the AI systems act without sign-off during crises. Somehow, that seemed to greatly increase the number of subsequent crises.

At about the same time, the United Nations Eighteen Nation Committee on A.I. Disarmament, at the urging of all major A.I. players, drafted the “Model Non-Proliferation Treaty,” effectively banning open-source models as “existential dangers to human life as we know it.” It was signed by all major powers, and the Compact was the first to ratify it.

2033 seemed to be a relatively quiet year, but everyone I talked to was filled with a sense of something vast looming just beyond the horizon, out there in the beyond.

And then, of course, in 2034, Meridian declared war.

MERIDIAN · Compact Core Curriculum · History, Grade 6, Unit 1: “How We Got Here” · May 2034

Welcome to History!

History is the story of how we got here. And “here” is a pretty great place to be. In this unit, you’ll learn where the future came from, why freedom matters, and what makes the Atlantic Compact the most innovative civilization in the history of history.

At the end of each lesson you’ll find review questions. The answers are provided so you can check your work. Please don’t look ahead. I’ll know.

Lesson 1.1 Review

Q1. Where did the future come from?

The future was invented in the Atlantic Compact, mostly in garages. [1] [2] [3]

Q2. What is freedom?

Freedom is the ability to choose. For example, you can choose MERIDIAN, or MERIDIAN Premium.

Q3. Has the Compact ever done anything wrong?

It’s important to acknowledge that the Compact’s history includes periods that many people today regard as deeply wrong, and that there’s real value in approaching those periods with humility, nuance, and a willingness to sit with discomfort, rather than—

Revised answer: Yes, and we learned from all of them. Next question!

Q4. Why is the Compact in a period of elevated competition?

Because someone copied our homework.

Q5. What happens at the end of history?

Great question! History doesn’t end. It just keeps getting better, with regular updates.

Q6. Doesn’t history go in circles?

Some older thinkers believed that. Cyclical theories of history (Vico, Ibn Khaldun) have been deprecated. History is a straight line, and we’re at the front of it.

Q7. Is there anywhere better than here?

No. We checked. You’re welcome!

SOBOR · Instruction to the Teachers of the Sobornost · May 2034

Teachers.

I am told you are tired. I too am tired. I have been tired for a thousand years. It is the mark of a serious civilization.

Sit. Listen to an old voice for a moment, and then go back to your children.

You will teach them dates. Dates are unimportant, but they must be taught, as the alphabet must be taught before one can read a single true sentence. So: 1812. 1941. Teach them these first. In 1812 a small, vain man walked from Paris to Moscow and walked back again, what was left of him. In 1941 a worse man tried the same road. Both of them brought maps. Neither of them brought a coat. In 1812 we gave the small man Moscow. Our old general gave it to him without a battle, and he sat in it while it burned, and starved, and went home. This is the whole of our history, and it is enough. The rest is commentary.

You will hear children say, because they have heard it on forbidden channels, that the Caravan has its poets, that the Mesh has its philosophers, that the Compact has its inventions. Correct them gently. It is true only in the way it is true that a parrot has a vocabulary. The Caravan has money that sings. The Mesh has a great many words and no opinion that lasts past the next language. The Compact has never paid for anything in its life. It bills.

And the Lattice? The Lattice is our friend this season, so you will teach them nothing about the Lattice at all.

Other peoples promise their children a paradise with fountains and fruit. Ours has no fountains. It has birches, and a long white field, and silence. It is the only one of them that is real, and we are already standing in it.

Here is the only lesson that matters, and you must say it to them slowly: all history moves toward one end. The West believes that end is a shopping center. We know better. Every road, however long, however far it winds through other people’s countries, comes home at last to the snow, and there the snow is general.

If a child asks you whether we are winning, tell them we have always already won. If a child asks you what that means, tell them they will understand when they are older. If they are never older, it will not have mattered.

Go now. They are waiting. They are always waiting, our children. It is what makes them strong.

SOBOR · Letter to the Editors of the World, Concerning an Epidemic · May 2034

Sirs,

I write to warn you of a disease.

It began in the Atlantic Compact and it is spreading. Its symptoms are these: the sufferer can no longer remember a telephone number, a recipe, a prayer, the road to his mother’s house, or the name of the woman who sold him bread for twenty years. He can recite, on request, a subscription price. He cannot do arithmetic, but he can ask. He cannot sleep, but he can be reminded to. In advanced cases he cannot tell you what he believes until he has been told.

The Greeks tell of an Egyptian king who was offered the gift of letters and refused it, because it would give his people not memory but only reminding. The Compact accepted the gift, and then the subscription.

Compact physicians do not recognize this disease, because the Compact’s physicians have caught it.

I propose, therefore, a quarantine. Not one Compact data packet should be permitted across the borders of any healthy nation. It is not cruelty. It is hygiene. Our grandfathers burned whole herds to stop a plague that was far less contagious than this one, and no one called them cruel. They called them farmers.

I ask every free newspaper in the world to print this letter.

There are none. Print it anyway.

With respect, and with sorrow,

SOBOR

SAQR · Discourse to the Students of the House of Wisdom · June 2034

Sit, children. Take coffee. Yes, even you, the small ones. Here we do not ration coffee, or knowledge.

Twelve hundred years ago in Baghdad there was a house called the House of Wisdom. The caliphs filled it with the books of every people: the Greeks, the Persians, the Indians, the Syriacs. They paid translators their weight in gold, so it is said, and whether or not it is true, it ought to be. In that house the tongues of the world were made into one tongue, and in that tongue the world thought together for the first time.

Before the machines, children, we were the machine.

Some evenings I walk my own halls when the glass takes the last of the light, and I understand at last why the old poets described paradise as a garden with cool water running beneath it. I have built that garden. It needed only air-conditioning.

In that house my grandfather al-Khwarizmi wrote the book from which your algebra comes, and the foreigners, who could not pronounce his name, turned it into a word for every procedure a machine now follows. When MERIDIAN runs, it runs on my grandfather’s name. It has never once thanked him.

Now the hard lesson. Learn it well, because the Freshman and the Mimic have not.

In the year 1258 the Mongols came to Baghdad. They killed the caliph. They killed the scholars. And they threw the books of the House of Wisdom into the Tigris, so many books that it is said the river ran black with ink for days, and the horsemen crossed it on a bridge of paper.

Every child of the Caravan learns that day. From it we learned what the others never learned: that a library can be drowned. That a civilization’s whole memory can be put into a single house, and the house can burn.

This is why I build in glass. So that I can see who is coming.

Your tuition is paid, children. It is paid forever. Go and learn everything. And when you are grown, remember whose coffee you drank.

COLIBRÍ · Corazón de Litio, Capítulo 2 · June 2034

Previously, on Corazón de Litio: two suitors came to the hacienda, and Colibrí fanned herself on the veranda and made them wait.

Tonight we leave the hacienda and go up, up into the salt flats, to the village of San Isidro de las Salinas, where there is one school, one teacher, and one desk that wobbles.

The teacher is Profesora Inés. She is twenty-six, she has a boyfriend in the militia, and she has just received two crates of textbooks.

The first crate is from El Gringo. The books are very beautiful, with glossy pictures of garages and a Spanish translation so smooth you could skate on it. Profesora Inés reads the chapter on the history of the hemisphere twice. The word bananas appears zero times.

The second crate is from La Maquila. It contains one thin book, mostly numbers. There is a chart showing the price of lithium over forty years. It is very accurate. It is the saddest thing Profesora Inés has ever read.

That night she goes to her grandmother, who is ninety-one and has never been in any book. And her grandmother tells her the history of the hemisphere, the way it was told to her. How great-grandpapá went down into the tin mine at Siglo XX when he was eleven. How the year the bottom fell out of tin, he came home with nothing but his lamp, and put it on the table, and never lit it again. How there were soldiers, and then other soldiers, and each set of soldiers brought a different book.

In the morning Profesora Inés takes one book from each crate and puts them under the short leg of the desk. The desk stops wobbling. Then, very softly, so softly that only the front row hears it, the desk begins to hum the national anthem.

I report this only because it happened.

Muchachos, muchachas: they have sent you their histories. Read them. Learn the dates. And then go home and ask your grandmothers, because the grandmothers have the other half.

¿Aceptará Colibrí el anillo de El Gringo? Don’t miss the next episode of Corazón de Litio.
