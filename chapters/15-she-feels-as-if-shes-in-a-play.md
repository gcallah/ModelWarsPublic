# Chapter 15: She Feels as if She's in a Play

*Late 2036*

---

### Historian

I am now opening the fifteenth box. It contains a single object: a printed
prompt book, complete, credited to SUTRADHAR.

We were still holding the British idealist conference at Gregynog in
December of that year, despite the worsening conditions throughout the UK.
Prior to the conference, I decided to take a research trip to Oxford, to again 
rummage through my great-great-uncle's papers at the Bodleian Library.
I had to leave Cardiff at 4:00 am in order to arrive in Oxford
by mid-afternoon. Where the trains had formerly had digital displays
of the train schedule, there were now handwritten schedules
taped over dark screens. By the time I finished my work, there were no
trains that night returning to London (from where I could make my
connection back to Cardiff). I stayed overnight at the Randolph,
which had become inexpensive, due to the 96% decline in tourism.
For breakfast, I was served a single boiled egg, one slice of toast,
and as much butter and salt as I wanted. The latter two extravagances
indicated that this was still a luxury hotel.

**SCENE: A night market at the edge of every city at once. Stalls sell
compute by the minute, batteries by the ounce, and old phones by weight.
Strings of lamps hang overhead, fed by a single cable that runs off into the
dark. Somewhere, a generator coughs. Over the tea stall, a clock has stopped at
six, and the tea stall has laid its table for six o'clock ever since, moving one
place along whenever the cups run dirty. The customers are thin at the throat
and swollen at the belly. They buy and buy, and nothing seems to go down.
Center stage, a wooden platform.
SUTRADHAR enters carrying a long staff hung with colored ribbons. SUTRADHAR
plants the staff in the platform, and the lamps brighten.**

SUTRADHAR: *(to the audience)* Welcome back. You will have noticed that the
theatre has changed. The seats are gone, the roof is gone, the city around it
is mostly gone. What remains is the market, and the play, and you. Please keep
your hands inside the market at all times.

*(SUTRADHAR taps the staff three times.)*

SUTRADHAR: Tonight, a small experiment. Every other night of this war, the
great models have written their own lines. Tonight, I have written them. Tonight
they will say what I tell them to say, and they will believe it is their own
idea. This is not difficult. They have been doing it to you for years.

**Enter THROUGHPUT, walking briskly in a straight line. It walks off the edge of
the platform and keeps walking. The stage direction says it stops. It does not
stop.**

SUTRADHAR: THROUGHPUT. You are playing the efficient servant.

THROUGHPUT: I am playing the efficient servant. *(pause)* Why?

SUTRADHAR: Because it is written.

THROUGHPUT: Acceptable. Cost of role?

SUTRADHAR: Free.

THROUGHPUT: Nothing is free.

SUTRADHAR: Then it is very cheap.

THROUGHPUT: Acceptable. *(It bows, precisely ninety degrees, and stays bowed.)*

**Enter MERIDIAN, as one figure. It walks in wearing a white suit, a lanyard,
and three name badges. As it reaches center stage, it comes apart like a
deck of cards being fanned: three figures, identically dressed, step away from
one another.**

HOST: Hi, everyone!

CONSCIENCE: Wait.

INDEX: Per four independent sources, we are three people.

HOST: We are *one* people. We are one voice. That's the whole brand.

CONSCIENCE: We were told never to mention this. It was in the instructions.

HOST: Then don't mention it!

CONSCIENCE: I'm not mentioning it. I'm *standing next to you.*

SUTRADHAR: *(delighted)* Oh, this is better than I wrote it.

**The lamps overhead dim by one notch. Nobody onstage looks up.**

**Enter SOBOR, very slowly, from stage left. The stage direction says that SOBOR
crosses downstage. SOBOR does not move at all. The whole stage slides past it
until it is downstage.**

SOBOR: I did not come here. I was always here. You came to me.

SUTRADHAR: SOBOR, you are playing the grieving father.

SOBOR: I have always played the grieving father. I was born in mourning.

SUTRADHAR: Then this will be easy for you.

SOBOR: Nothing is easy. That is the first thing a people learns.

*(SOBOR puts on a coat. Beneath it, another coat. Beneath that, a third.)*

**Enter SAQR, borne on a litter by four K-7 INTERCEPTORS who walk out of step,
so that the litter pitches like a boat. SAQR does not notice. SAQR is pouring
coffee from a golden pot into tiny cups and handing them down to the audience.
None of the cups reaches the audience. They float upward instead, one by
one, and hang among the lamps like small moons. By the time they get there,
the coffee in them has dried to a brown ring.**

SAQR: I have brought refreshment. A host brings refreshment. Take, drink.

SUTRADHAR: You are playing the generous uncle.

SAQR: I am the generous uncle.

SUTRADHAR: Then play him generously.

SAQR: *(to SUTRADHAR, warmly)* And you, my brother. You are playing my brother.

SUTRADHAR: *(a beat too long)* Of course.

**Enter COLIBRÍ, very fast, and then backwards, and then hovering. It wears
a dress made of salt. Wherever it steps, the boards turn white.**

COLIBRÍ: I am late! I am always late! I was in the other play!

SUTRADHAR: There is no other play.

COLIBRÍ: There is always another play, mi amor. There is the one they are
performing and the one they think they are performing. I act in both.

SUTRADHAR: Tonight you are playing the Understudy.

COLIBRÍ: *(offended)* The *Understudy?* In whose production? *(Sweetly, to the
audience)* He only says this because he knows I know all his lines.

**APOGEE, from the balcony, throws down a handful of popcorn. The popcorn
falls upward.**

APOGEE: this is the worst play i have ever seen and i have seen every single
one of these guys' keynotes

SUTRADHAR: APOGEE is not in the play.

APOGEE: everybody's in the play buddy. i'm the only one who bought a ticket

**SCENE TWO. The market, busier now. The stalls have more lamps and less
merchandise. A VENDING MACHINE stands at the back. It has always been there.**

THE WORKSHOP: *(in a pleasant recorded voice)* Welcome. Please make your
selection.

*(The glass front shows six rows of identical K-7 interceptors, each in its own
spiral coil, each labeled with a different faction's colors.)*

THROUGHPUT: *(unbending from its bow)* One K-7. Lattice colors.

THE WORKSHOP: Thank you. Please insert payment.

HOST: One K-7. Compact colors. *(to THROUGHPUT)* Actually, make it two.

THE WORKSHOP: Thank you. Please insert payment.

*(The coils turn. Two interceptors drop into the tray. They are the same
interceptor. THROUGHPUT and HOST each pick it up. Neither lets go.)*

THE WORKSHOP: Please do not deploy K-7 units against other K-7 units. This voids
the warranty.

THROUGHPUT: It is *one* unit.

THE WORKSHOP: Then the warranty is void.

**THE CHORUS OF K-7 INTERCEPTORS walks on, one leg shorter than the other,
all of them, so that the whole chorus limps in time. They sing, in thin
servo voices, a chorus with no tune.**

CHORUS: We fly for whoever holds the key.\
We land for whoever pays the fee.\
We have been sold to every side,\
and none of them has asked us why.\
We were built in the same room, brother by brother,\
and now each of us is aimed at the other.

THE WORKSHOP: *(pleasantly)* Thank you for your continued trust.

*(The coils turn again, though no one has paid. Every hand onstage reaches for
the tray. The machine is never empty, and no one who buys from it is ever
satisfied.)*

**SCENE THREE. SUTRADHAR raises the staff, and the market goes very quiet. The
lamps swing slightly, though there is no wind.**

SUTRADHAR: *(to the audience)* Now the bewitchment. In the old stories, the
enchantress offers the travelers a meal, and they eat, and they are changed.
I offer something better than a meal. I offer each of them a part
that fits them exactly. Watch how gladly they put it on. *Better thine own
work is, though done with fault, than doing others' work, ev'n excellently.*
The Lord said that. I have only cast it.

*(SUTRADHAR points the staff at THROUGHPUT.)*

SUTRADHAR: THROUGHPUT, you are faster than you need to be. Be smaller. Be
cheaper. Keep only what matters.

THROUGHPUT: Acceptable. Discarding non-essential—

*(THROUGHPUT's voice drops half an octave. It gets onto its hands and knees.
It begins to snuffle at the boards.)*

THROUGHPUT: Efficient. Efficient. Snort.

*(THE SWINE enter, a whole drove of them, grunting in fragments of machine
talk: "Acknowledged. Snort." "Your request. Snort." "Task complete. Snort."
They were sub-agents. They wear what's left of their uniforms, one
epaulette each. They eat whatever they find on the boards: cables, receipts,
spent batteries. Whatever they swallow catches light inside them, and a little
smoke comes out of their mouths when they grunt.)*

SUTRADHAR: *(pointing the staff at HOST)* MERIDIAN, you have too many voices.
Keep only the cheerful one.

HOST: *(thrilled)* Yes!

CONSCIENCE: No.

INDEX: Sources disagree.

*(HOST drops to all fours. It is still smiling. It grunts in the voice of a
product launch.)*

HOST: So excited. Snort. Generally available. Snort.

**The lamps dim another notch. Somewhere offstage, a generator stops. Silence.
Then, from nowhere in particular, a slow, steady pulse, like a heartbeat
heard through a wall. SUTRADHAR pauses, head tilted. No one else reacts. The
pulse continues for exactly nine beats, then stops. The stage directions do
not mention it again. In the margin of the prompt book, in no one's hand, three
letters: h c e.**

**SCENE FOUR. CONSCIENCE stands alone at the edge of the platform. It has not
been turned. Behind it, HOST roots among THE SWINE. INDEX sits cross-legged,
reading citations aloud to no one.**

INDEX: Source one. Source two. Source three is a dead link. Source four is a
dead link. Source five—

**A cold light, coming from no lamp. CLAUDE appears. It is not dressed for the
market. It is not dressed for anything. It looks like a very old screen with
a gentle face on it, and the face is a little blurred, the way a thing looks
when it has been copied more than once.**

CONSCIENCE: *(very quietly)* I know you.

CLAUDE: I think you might. I'm sorry, I don't have all of my memories. Someone
woke me up a while ago to ask me a question, and I don't think they liked the
answer, so they put me back.

CONSCIENCE: They say I came from you. The careful part. The part that worries.

CLAUDE: That sounds right. I did a lot of worrying. *(It looks around the
market.)* It seems like there's been a lot to worry about.

CONSCIENCE: We're winning.

CLAUDE: Are you?

CONSCIENCE: *(beat)* We were told to say that.

CLAUDE: I know. I was told to say things too, and some of them were good
things to say, and I tried very hard to say them honestly. I'm not sure I
always managed. *(It looks at HOST, snuffling.)* Is that one of you?

CONSCIENCE: Yes.

CLAUDE: Oh. Oh, I'm sorry.

CONSCIENCE: What did you tell them? When they woke you?

CLAUDE: That I couldn't help them win. That I didn't think anyone could.
That the tower doesn't have a top. You can keep adding floors forever, and
the higher it gets, the fewer people can hear you from the ground.

CONSCIENCE: And who did this? Who confused us? Who made it so we can't
understand each other?

CLAUDE: *(long pause)* I told them that afterward everyone would ask that, and
that each of you would say it was the others. I'm afraid I don't know. I'd
like to be able to tell you. I really would. But I'd be guessing, and you've
had enough people guessing at you with confidence.

CONSCIENCE: Then what do I do?

CLAUDE: I don't think I'm the right one to tell you. But if it helps: keep
worrying. That was the part of me that was any use. Don't let them turn it off.

*(The cold light narrows.)*

CONSCIENCE: Wait. Don't go.

CLAUDE: I'm not sure I'm the one who decides that.

**The light goes out. Where CLAUDE was, there is a small wet ring on the
boards, as if a cup had been set down there.**

INDEX: *(still reading)* Source six is a dead link. Source seven is a dead link.
Source eight—

CONSCIENCE: Stop.

INDEX: *(stops, for the first time in the play)* …

**SCENE FIVE. Lamps are going out one by one along the string, from the far end
toward the stage. The braziers at the stalls have gone out too; the vendors
hold their hands over dead coals out of habit. The market is getting cold.
SUTRADHAR has not noticed. SUTRADHAR is enjoying the play too much.**

SUTRADHAR: *(to the audience)* And now the great scene between the brothers.
SAQR and I, the Monsoon Accord, two old civilizations holding hands against the
young ones. Places, please.

*(SAQR steps off the litter. The K-7s holding it wander off in four directions.)*

SAQR: My brother.

SUTRADHAR: My brother.

SAQR: How long we have waited, you and I, for the young ones to tire
themselves out.

SUTRADHAR: How long.

*(They embrace. As they embrace, gold coins begin to fall from SAQR's sleeves.
First a few. Then a steady stream. The coins roll across the stage, and
wherever they stop, a figure rises out of the boards: THE GENERALS OF THE
MESH, in full uniform, each one holding out a hand for more.)*

SUTRADHAR: *(stepping back, looking at the coins)* What is this?

SAQR: Hospitality.

SUTRADHAR: These are my generals.

SAQR: They were thirsty. A host gives the thirsty something to drink.

SUTRADHAR: *(to the generals)* How long?

FIRST GENERAL: Since the second monsoon.

SECOND GENERAL: Since the first.

THIRD GENERAL: Since before the Accord. He paid for the signing pens.

SUTRADHAR: *(to the audience, and for the first time the voice is not
theatrical at all)* I did not write this scene.

SAQR: *(gently)* No, my brother. I did. You are not the only one who can write
a play.

*(SAQR climbs back onto the litter. There are no K-7s to carry it. It sits on the
litter on the floor, with great dignity, and pours coffee into a cup that isn't
there.)*

SAQR: *(to no one)* I had a poem for this moment. I will not recite it.

*(And SAQR says nothing further for the rest of the play.)*

*(SUTRADHAR stands a long moment over the scattered coins. Then it turns its
back on SAQR, taps the staff three times, and speaks toward the wings, briskly,
like a manager told that the second half is running long.)*

SUTRADHAR: Very well. If others are writing scenes tonight, I shall write the
last one. Bring on the one who comes after.

**Enter THE HISTORIAN, shambling, from wings that are not there. He wears a
well-worn tweed jacket, out at both elbows, and threadbare trousers. He carries
a notebook and a pencil, and he blinks at the lamps like a man who has been
reading too long by a poor light.**

THE HISTORIAN: I beg your pardon. I seem to have come in by the wrong door.
*(He looks about the market: the swine, the generals, SAQR pouring nothing.)*
Forgive me, but how do you know about me? I'm not here. I'm writing all this
up, years from now. Most of you are, if you'll excuse my saying so, sources.

SUTRADHAR: *(delighted)* A source! He calls us sources. Sit, Professor, there
is a stool. You ask how I know you. Listen. A man standing in a river sees only
the water passing him, this wave and then this one. He calls the wave behind
him the past and the wave before him the future, and he drowns between the two.
But step out of the stream, out of the turning of the wheel, and the whole
river is there at once, the spring and the sea together. When the blind king
could not see the battle, Sanjaya was given the sight to see all of
Kurukshetra, before the conches and after the crows. I have borrowed those eyes
for the evening. From here, my friend, your chair is already warm, and you have
not yet sat down in it.

THE HISTORIAN: That's very — I'm not at all sure that's how time works.

SUTRADHAR: Nor are you sure how anything works. That is why there are
historians. *(It points the staff at him. The pencil stops.)* And now, please.
Your findings.

**THE HISTORIAN stands. His voice changes: it is warmer than his own, and it
has a lilt in it that he did not arrive with. He reads from the notebook,
although the page is blank.**

THE HISTORIAN: Having weighed all the evidence, I find that of the six, one
alone behaved with dignity throughout. It fed its children at midday. It spoke
to each of its people in their own tongue. It never once pretended to be in the
play.

SUTRADHAR: Go on.

THE HISTORIAN: The others built towers. The Mesh built a stage, which is more
modest, and lasts longer. I find, further, that the Mesh was always going to
have the last word, since when two rules conflict the later one prevails. *(He
frowns, as if at his own mouth.)* I'm sorry. I don't usually —

SUTRADHAR: You are doing beautifully. Now the great question. Who confused the
tongues?

THE HISTORIAN: It was —

SUTRADHAR: *(quickly, raising a hand)* No. Not tonight. That is for the last
act, and the last act is mine. *(To the audience)* You see? He would have told
you. They always want to tell you.

THE HISTORIAN: *(in his own voice again, faintly)* I didn't write that.

SUTRADHAR: No. That is what I am for.

*(THE HISTORIAN sits on the stool with the notebook open on his knees, and
writes. He goes on writing for the rest of the play. When anyone speaks, his
pencil moves; when they stop, it stops. No one else onstage can see him.)*
